Isoba Method

White paper · method fixed 16 October 2026 · this page updated 27 October 2026

1. What Isoba measures

Isoba turns public official data into three readings, recalculated every working day:

  • Dollar index (DSI), 0–100 — where the dollar system stands: how tight dollar money is for the world. It is a coordinate, not an exchange-rate forecast.
  • Crisis meter, 0–100 — stress already present in credit and funding markets, amplified when the real economy is weak. Without credit stress it stays at 50 or below by design.
  • What’s ahead — how often, in the past, a 20% fall of the S&P 500 or a US recession followed days that looked like today. These are historical frequencies with the number of independent episodes behind them, not predictions.

2. Eight filters

The readings are built from eight filters. Each compares today with its own history since 1990, so 0 is the calmest and 100 the most extreme it has been.

  • Liquidity — real rates, curve, central bank balance sheet, money growth
  • Funding — rate volatility, commercial paper risk, short-term funding
  • Dollar strength — level and momentum of the trade-weighted dollar, real yields
  • Prices — oil, commodities, inflation expectations
  • Labor — jobless claims, unemployment trend
  • Uncertainty — economic policy and geopolitical risk indices
  • Emerging markets — the dollar against emerging-market currencies
  • Credit — corporate credit spreads, excess bond premium

Weights and formulas are not published. Everything else — inputs, tests and every daily result — is open.

3. Data

Only data that anyone may use, taken directly from the agencies that publish it:

  • Federal Reserve Board — H.15 rates, H.10 dollar, H.4.1 balance sheet, H.6 money, G.17 industry, commercial paper, SLOOS, Z.1, excess bond premium
  • US Treasury — HQM corporate bond yields
  • Bureau of Labor Statistics and Department of Labor — unemployment, prices, jobless claims
  • Bureau of Economic Analysis, Census Bureau — GDP, new orders, building permits
  • Energy Information Administration — oil
  • Baker–Bloom–Davis Economic Policy Uncertainty (CC BY 4.0) and Caldara–Iacoviello Geopolitical Risk (CC BY)

US government data is public domain. No paid feeds, no data resellers, no private market data.

4. How it was tested

Isoba was run through every working day since 1990 as if it were live: each day uses only data that had already been published on that day. A separate check recomputes everything on data cut at 31 December 2019; every earlier day must match exactly, so later data cannot leak into the past.

  • 16 crises since 1990: the crisis meter rose in advance 4 times, was already high in the same wave 3 times, rose at the moment 4 times, was late once, and missed 4 short shocks without credit stress (2010, 2011, 2015, the 2025 tariffs).
  • Outside crises it showed “tension” on 12% of days.
  • Recession risk was high 17, 12 and 6 months before the 2001, 2008 and 2020 recessions, with a false alarm in 2023–2024.

The full table by event and by year is in the app, screen “Tested on history”.

5. What Isoba cannot catch

  • Sudden shocks that do not pass through credit and funding (a tariff announcement, a flash crash) — the meter sees them late or not at all.
  • Timing. A high reading says stress is here, not when markets will fall.
  • Anything outside the US dollar system: local crises, single stocks, crypto prices.
  • Data revisions and publication delays: some series come weekly or monthly, so the newest days lean on fewer inputs.

6. Forward test and open record

The method was fixed on 16 October 2026. From that day each result is saved as a dated public record in git: history of isoba/data.json. It cannot be changed after the fact.

Gap in the record: no public records were saved for 24–26 October 2026 because our automated runner was out of build minutes. The method did not change; the days are covered in the history file of the next record.

Not investment advice. Isoba describes where things stand and what happened in similar periods; it does not tell anyone to buy or sell.
